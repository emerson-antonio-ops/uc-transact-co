The question the whole week answers

How much revenue did we make yesterday — and why should I trust that number?

The CFO · TransactCo
The hidden split

One question. Two different problems.

The request sounds like arithmetic. It is actually a question about meaning, provenance, and validation.

1 · Calculate a number

The easy problem
  • Sum some column, filter some dates
  • Any plausible query produces a figure
  • Precise to the cent — instantly
  • Nothing about it says it is right

2 · Establish trust in the number

The real problem — today's focus
  • Which business event recognizes revenue?
  • Which statuses count? How do refunds behave?
  • Which timezone defines "yesterday"?
  • Who owns each semantic decision?
The teaching thesis
A report can be precise and still be wrong. Foundation Investigation attacks the second problem before anyone builds an analytical system for the first.

1.

The System

A brownfield is not an empty repository. It has history, implicit rules, constraints, and existing users — and tonight, it has a store to protect.

CONCRETE TENSION FIRST — CONCEPTS ONLY AFTER
The inherited system

TransactCo — a store and its analytics, sharing one database

High-volume e-commerce. The operational workload runs the store; the analytical workload scans it to explain it. Both compete for the same Postgres.

Act 1 · the proof

A plausible query is not yet a revenue definition

The audience leaves Act 1 able to restate the tension, name the four entities, and explain why this query proves nothing:

-- precise to the cent. meaning: undefined. SELECT SUM(total_amount) FROM orders WHERE ordered_at::date = 'yesterday'; -- which statuses count? unknown -- refunds? unhandled -- paid vs ordered? conflated -- whose timezone? nobody decided

What "trustworthy" requires

A metric, not just a number
  • Defined meaning — which business event recognizes revenue
  • Provenance — which sources and columns back the figure
  • Validation — evidence the definition holds in the data
  • Ownership — a human accountable for each semantic choice
ACT 2 · 19:15 · WHY PROMPTS MATTER

2.

The Prompt

A prompt is a contract for the next piece of work — not a wish, not a knowledge base, not a vibe.

VAGUE REQUEST ≠ EXECUTABLE ASSIGNMENT
Demonstration A vs B · run both, live

The weak ask vs the investigation contract

> Analyze this database and explain revenue. what the room will catch: ✗ assumptions presented as facts ✗ no evidence required ✗ revenue semantics undefined ✗ no authority boundary ✗ no stopping condition
> Investigate how TransactCo represents revenue. > Use the repository and read-only database access. > Identify entities, relationships, invariants, > risks, and unresolved business questions. > Support every claim with a path, schema object, > or query. Label facts, inferences, decisions, > and open questions. Do not modify the database > or code. Stop and ask when a conclusion requires > business authority rather than technical evidence.
The proof of Act 2
The audience can explain why each clause exists — and predict exactly which failure it prevents.
The anatomy

Eight clauses of an investigation prompt

The prompt does not carry domain knowledge. It tells the agent what work to perform with the context and tools it receives.

01
Objective
The single outcome this piece of work must produce.
02
Context
What world the agent is given — and its provenance.
03
Questions
The specific things the investigation must answer.
04
Tools
What is permitted — repository reads, read-only queries.
05
Authority
Inspect vs modify vs decide. Never implicit.
06
Evidence
The standard every important claim must meet.
07
Output
The artifact's shape — so review needs no chat replay.
08
Stop
When to halt and escalate to a human owner.
ACT 3 · 19:40 · WHY CONTEXT MATTERS

3.

The Context

The prompt defines the work. Context supplies the world. The model knows what an order usually means — not what TransactCo recognizes as revenue.

SELECTION · ORDERING · PROVENANCE — NEVER A FILE DUMP
The layers

Six layers of context — each answers a different question

Business
Why does the work matter?
The CFO's question, the store at risk, the stakes.
Domain
What do the terms mean here?
Revenue, refund, "yesterday" — as TransactCo uses them.
System
Where do things live?
Code, data, interfaces — the repository map and DDL.
Operational
What must stay safe?
The store keeps running; the investigation stays read-only.
Evidence
What can support a claim?
Schema objects, sample rows, safe queries.
Authority
What may the agent decide?
Inspect, change, or decide — each explicitly granted.
Context engineering
Selection, ordering, and provenance. Not copying every file into a model window.
Demonstration · build the inventory live

A deliberate package, a cited ledger

The context package

Every source included for a reason
  • Business brief · repository map
  • Postgres DDL · data dictionary
  • Representative, non-sensitive sample rows
  • Operational constraints · read-only connection
  • Technical-brief template · telemetry contract

The evidence ledger

Every important claim gets a label
  • Fact — directly supported by evidence
  • Inference — reasonable, not conclusively established
  • Decision — chosen by someone with authority
  • Question — needs more evidence or an owner
The proof of Act 3
The audience can point to the exact context source that changed or supported a specific conclusion.

Why the last edge is dashed
Whether — and when — a payment becomes recognized revenue is a business decision. Not something the model should invent. The dashes are the whole lesson.
The vocabulary

Six words that make meaning portable

Entity
Something the business cares about
Customer, product, order, payment.
Relationship
How entities connect
Inferred from names, values, cardinalities — then tested.
Rule
What must remain true
Invariants and reconciliations, with scope and evidence.
Event
Something that happened in business time
Ordered, paid — anchored to the domain clock.
Provenance
The source behind the statement
A path, schema object, or query — always attached.
Owner
Who is authorized to decide meaning
Unresolved semantics get an owner, not a guess.
The proof of Act 4
Every ontology edge is evidenced, explicitly inferred, or marked as an unresolved business decision. No silent promotions.
The trap inside "yesterday"

Three clocks tick in every row

They answer different questions — and must never be silently substituted for one another.

Business time

ordered_at · paid_at
  • When it happened in the domain
  • The clock the CFO is asking about

Lifecycle time

created_at · updated_at
  • When the source system touched the record
  • The operational system's clock

Ingestion time

ingested_at
  • When the row reached this database
  • The clock late-arriving data lives on
Deliberately unresolved
Which timezone defines "yesterday"? How does late-arriving data change a published number? These need an owner — not an assumption.

ACT 6 · 21:35 · WHY DOCUMENTATION MATTERS

6.

The Brief

Knowledge that exists only in a chat session cannot guide the next module. Evidence becomes valuable when it survives the session.

REVIEWABLE WITHOUT REPLAYING THE CHAT
The durable artifact

One brief, thirteen sections, one review gate

What goes in

technical brief · v1
  • Business problem · system boundary · data inventory
  • Ontology v1 · confirmed rules · evidence ledger
  • Risks · assumptions · open questions with owners
  • Proposed analytical boundary · success measures
  • Non-goals · ADR candidates

The review gate

checked by a human, not vibes
  • Claims evidenced or explicitly uncertain
  • Business decisions have accountable owners
  • Boundary is read-only and one-directional
  • Nothing authorizes later-module implementation
  • Human review is recorded
ADR candidates — not ADRs
The investigation identifies decisions. A decision becomes an ADR only when choice, alternatives, trade-offs, evidence, and an accountable owner are explicit.
